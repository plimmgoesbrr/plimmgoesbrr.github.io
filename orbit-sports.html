Spin doctors

Sport looks like strength and speed. Look closer and it's physics: spinning balls bending through the air, and cameras that know where a ball landed better than any human eye.

// the magnus effect

Why a spinning ball bends

A spinning ball drags a thin layer of air around with it. On one side, the ball's surface moves along with the air rushing past; on the other, it moves against it. That makes the pressure uneven, so the ball gets pushed toward the side moving along with the air. That push is the Magnus effect.

  • Topspin (top of the ball rolling forward) pushes the ball down. You can hit it hard and high over the net, and it still dives into the court.
  • Backspin (slice) pushes it up, so it floats, stays low and skids off the bounce.
  • Sidespin curves it left or right, which is how a serve swings away from a returner.

// the goats

Rafa then, Alcaraz now

Rafael Nadal

The king of topspin. His forehand spun so hard it kicked up above opponents' shoulders, and it carried him to 14 French Open titles, a record that may never be broken. Pure grit with a whip-like swing.

Carlos Alcaraz

World No. 1 at 19, the youngest ever. He can crush a forehand from the baseline and then, one point later, float a drop shot that dies on the other side of the net. Power and touch in the same player.

// computer vision on court

How Hawk-Eye calls a line

This is where tennis meets the stuff I build. Hawk-Eye uses around ten high-speed cameras placed around the court:

  1. Spot the ballEach camera finds the ball in every frame, hundreds of times a second.
  2. TriangulateTwo cameras seeing the same ball from different angles pin down where it is in 3D, the same way your two eyes judge distance.
  3. Fit the flightThe 3D points are joined into a smooth path, accounting for gravity, drag and spin.
  4. Find the bounceWhere the path meets the ground, including how the ball squashes on impact, decides in or out to within a few millimetres.
